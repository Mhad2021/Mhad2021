# whichaisent.com — content batch 01

Product: AI referral attribution. Shows which AI assistant sent a visitor or
lead. Audience: small business owners and marketers/agencies.

The angle throughout: the channel is already sending traffic and the owner is
misreading it as something else. That is a live, checkable error rather than a
prediction, which is why it does not need statistics to land.

Constraints held: no invented statistics, no fabricated case studies, no fake
urgency, no free-audit CTA, no category jargon, one em dash maximum per post.
Every post is a different structure.

---

## Instagram

### IG-1 — the misread
Your direct traffic went up and you put it down to word of mouth.

Maybe. Or an AI recommended you to someone, they typed your name straight into
the browser, and your analytics had nowhere to file it.

Those two things look identical in a report. They are not the same business.

One of them can stop next month and never tell you why.

### IG-2 — the thirty-second check
Thirty-second job.

Open your analytics. Go to referrers. Search for chatgpt.com.

Most owners have never once looked. Whatever is sitting there, you have been
running the business without it.

### IG-3 — two visitors
Two people land on your site today.

One clicked a Google result and is comparing you against four others.

The other was told by an AI that you're the one to call, and typed your name in
themselves.

Your dashboard counts both as a visitor. On the phone they are nothing alike.

If you can't tell them apart, your conversion rate is an average of two things
with nothing in common.

## Facebook

### FB-1 — the misread, longer
Your direct traffic is up. Nice. Most owners file that under word of mouth and
move on.

Here's the other explanation.

Someone asked an AI for a recommendation. It named you. They didn't click
anything, they just typed your name into the browser, because that's what people
do when they've already been told who to call.

No referrer. No campaign. Nothing for your analytics to attach it to. So it
lands in direct, next to the people who genuinely remembered you.

Word of mouth builds slowly and rarely collapses. A machine's recommendation can
change the week a model updates, and you would watch the number fall without
ever knowing what moved.

Same line on the graph. Completely different thing to be exposed to.

### FB-2 — for the agencies
If you run marketing for other people, this one's going to sting.

You cannot invoice a flat line.

When an AI starts recommending your client, the leads arrive and your dashboard
shows nothing. No channel, no campaign, no attribution. The client sees a quiet
report and starts wondering what they're paying for, while the thing actually
working sits in a column called direct.

You're not just losing credit for it. You're losing the argument about budget,
because budget follows whatever has numbers next to it.

## LinkedIn

### LI-1 — the attribution gap, for marketers
Every channel you report on earned its place by producing a number.

Paid has a number. Organic has a number. Email has a number. They get reviewed,
argued over and funded on the strength of those numbers.

AI referrals mostly don't produce one. Someone reads your client's name in an
answer, types it into the browser, and arrives with no referrer attached. It
lands in direct, which is the column nobody optimises because nobody can.

So the channel that sends the most pre-sold traffic is the one channel with no
case for budget. Not because it performs badly. Because it reports badly.

That gap is going to decide a lot of marketing arguments over the next two years,
and most teams haven't noticed it's open.

### LI-2 — the lead quality point
There's a difference between a visitor who found you and a visitor who was
recommended to you, and your analytics does not record it.

The first clicked a result and is actively comparing you to alternatives. The
second was told by something they trust that you are the right choice, and came
looking for you by name.

They close at different rates. They ask different questions. They negotiate
differently.

Averaged together, they produce a conversion rate that describes neither, and a
cost-per-lead that quietly misprices both.
